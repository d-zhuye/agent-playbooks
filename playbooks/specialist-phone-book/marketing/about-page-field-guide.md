# About page field guide

The knowledge behind the [About page specialist](about-page.md). It was compiled on 2026-11-20
from two web research passes. The first asked what good About pages do and how experienced
writers make them. The second asked what bad About pages do, which popular rules fail, and what
an inexperienced workflow looks like. The two passes were run separately and then cross-checked:
they agreed wherever they overlapped, including on every traced statistic. Read the brief first.
Use this guide to ground each judgment, and cite it by section.

**Grades.** A = peer-reviewed or large-sample study with its method shown. B = a research firm,
platform owner, standards body, regulator text or enforcement action with a stated method or
binding force (NN/g, Stanford, Google, W3C, EU and US law, Apple, Google Play, FTC). C = a
practitioner case study or test with data shown, or investigative journalism backed by documents.
D = opinion, a vendor blog, or a statistic with no traceable source. Most About page research is
on desktop and on B2B sites. Consumer and phone behaviour on About pages is barely studied.

**Dates matter.** Laws, store rules, Google's guidelines, enforcement and AI assistants change.
Before quoting a rule or number from this guide as current, check its source again. Section 18
lists those sources.

## 1. What an About page is, and isn't

- **An About page is a role, not a label.** It's the page whose main job is to say who is behind a
  website, what they do, where they are and how to reach a person, in a form a visitor can check.
  The role is older than the label: Berners-Lee's "Sign it!" rule (W3C style guide, 1994) asked
  every author for a page with their mail address and phone number, linked from every file, and
  called it the place for legal notices [B]. Identity, contact and legal notices are still the
  core. Story, values and design serve them.
- **The layers since then:** a signature page, then a trust page (Nielsen's "Trust or Bust" 1999,
  Stanford 2002), then an entity page read by machines (Google's "About this result" 2021, E-E-A-T
  2022, AI assistants 2024 on). Some About pages now open with a note to AI agents (Linear, checked
  2026-11-20) [C]. Each layer added jobs without removing the earlier ones.
- **The variants, each with its own job:** About or About us (the umbrella), Our story or
  history, founder letter or meet the maker, team or leadership, mission, values or manifesto,
  company or corporate information, imprint or legal notice (Impressum), colophon, humans.txt,
  disclosures, transparency or financials, press kit, careers, author or profile page, and
  "About the game" or product. Small makers usually combine several on one page. Large ones
  split them under one hub [B, NN/g; C, real pages].
- **Things that look like an About page but fail its job:**
  - **A mission or values page** that never says who runs it, what it does, where it is or how to
    reach them. NN/g's observers "saw journalists, investors, and general web users baffled" by
    such pages [B, NN/g 2019]. "When the tagline could equally well describe thousands of other
    companies, you know you're in trouble" [B, NN/g 2015].
  - **A press bio:** third person, credentials first, written for "why should I quote them?", not
    "can I trust them?" It belongs in the press kit.
  - **A sales pitch** that keeps steering to the product or a call. Readers reclassify it as
    persuasion and discount it [A, Friestad & Wright 1994].
  - **A team-photo grid with no story,** which says nothing about what the maker does.
  - **An invented or sharpened origin story:** a liability on a delay (section 8).
  - **A careers page,** which answers job seekers only.
  - **An imprint alone,** which meets the law and not trust. And the reverse: a rich story with no
    legal identity doesn't meet the law where it applies [B].
  - **"About" used for the product** (common for games), with the maker nowhere. Visitors looking
    for who made it don't find them unless About links to the studio or press kit.

## 2. Why it became standard, and when it isn't needed

Three separate systems ask "who is behind this?", and each reinforces the others.

- **People.** Of the ten Stanford web credibility guidelines, five are About page jobs: make it
  easy to verify, show a real organization (an address is the easiest proof), show expertise,
  show honest people stand behind the site, make contact easy [B, Fogg 2002, over 4,500
  participants across three years of studies].
- **Search quality.** Google's raters are told to "look at the 'About us' page … as a starting
  point", then to look for independent reviews and news, and "when there is disagreement between
  what the website or content creators say about themselves and what reputable independent sources
  say, trust the independent sources" [B, rater guidelines 2025-09-11]. Fabricated creator
  profiles, made-up names and AI headshots are named as deception.
- **Law and app stores.** EU e-Commerce Directive Art. 5 requires name, geographic address, email
  and register details to be "easily, directly and permanently accessible". Germany's § 5 DDG has
  carried this since 2024-05-14 (it replaced § 5 TMG), and the UK has SI 2015/17 for companies.
  Apple shows EU traders' address, phone and email on product pages and removed apps without
  trader status from 2025-02-17. Google Play shows verified developer identity. The About page
  now sits one tap from a legal record the visitor can see [B].
- **Site software ships it as the default.** WordPress's sample page still says "Most people start
  with an About page" [B]. Visitors expect one, so a missing page is itself a signal, and template
  text left in place is a worse one.

**Identity is a threshold factor, not a headline.** Asked directly, people rate an address
(+1.86 on −3 to +3), phone (+1.71) and email (+1.53) as strong credibility boosters, and staff
photos weakly (+0.69) [A, Fogg 2001, n=1,410, data from 2000]. When judging real sites, only
8.8% of comments mention who runs the site: 28.9% for nonprofits, about 10% for shops, finance
and health, 2.6% for entertainment [A, Fogg 2002, n=2,684]. People rarely think about identity
until it's missing or the stakes are high. A fact only counts if it's noticed
(prominence-interpretation theory) [A, Fogg 2003].

**When it isn't needed, or does harm:**

- **Google requires findable responsibility, not a page called About,** scaled to the stakes:
  "humor websites may not need the level of detailed contact information we would expect from
  online banking websites", personal sites may omit a home address, and an FAQ can do the About
  page's job [B, rater guidelines §2.5.2–2.5.3]. Sites that handle money, data or health with no
  information about who runs them are rated Lowest.
- **A box-ticking page can cost more than none.** Fraud shops copy About text from real shops,
  "shared among them with minimum change" [A, SCAMMAGNIFIER, NDSS 2025, 46,746 suspected fraud
  shops]. Generic copy now looks like a scam's. A page that answers nothing sends visitors to
  search, where they meet reviews and rivals [B, NN/g].
- **The job may be done better elsewhere.** A footer line, the store listing or the home page
  may carry identity for low-stakes sites. Map where each identity fact already appears before
  adding a page (section 11).

## 3. Who opens it, when, and what they expect

**Few start there, and many need it at the decision point.** In the one survey with data, 16% of
262 B2B buyers looked at About first, 47% went to products and 33% to the home page. 52% wanted
About reachable from the home page, 44% leave sites without contact information, and 46% leave
when they "can't tell what a company does" [C, KoMarketing 2015, self-selected, 2014 data]. No
credible public data exists on About pages' share of traffic. Get the site's own numbers.

**The visitor checks the maker off-site and believes what they find there.** NN/g users compared
company claims with Google Reviews, Yelp and Glassdoor, and said "At this point, I'd just Google
it". Its latest round found users "more skeptical than in the past" [B, NN/g 2019]. The FTC tells
shoppers to search a seller's name with "complaint" or "scam" [B]. The About page wins by agreeing
with that check, not by praising itself.

| Reader | What brings them, and when | What they look for |
|---|---|---|
| New visitor near a decision (install, buy, sign up) | The product caught their interest; now they check the maker | Who, what, where, a human route, how it makes money |
| Skeptic checking for a scam | A deal, an unknown brand, a link from a stranger | Checkable facts that match outside sources; any sign of fakery |
| Gatekeeper choosing for someone else (parent, teacher, procurement, privacy reviewer) | Before letting others use it | Accountability: legal entity, contact, data practices, who it's for |
| Existing user | Something went wrong, or curiosity | A person to reach; whether it will last |
| Sharer | About to recommend it | Something they can stand behind |
| Journalist, creator | Writing about it | Dates, names, location, numbers, press contact, a quotable line |
| Investor, partner, job seeker | Due diligence | Leadership, ownership, scale, culture |
| App reviewer | Store review | Developer identity and contact that match the listing |
| Search engines and raters | Building an entity picture | Plain statements, structured data, consistency with other sources |
| AI assistants | Answering "who makes this?" | Plain, extractable facts. They lean mostly on third parties (one vendor count: about 4.5% of brand citations came from the brand's own About, FAQ or home pages) [D] and hallucinate more on little-known makers [A] |
| Link-preview bots | The page shared in a chat | The page's own og:title, og:description and og:image [B] |

- **Write for a polite skeptic,** someone who will check one claim and leave if it fails. Makers
  picture fans. The readers who open About are often deciding whether the maker is real [A, B].
- **Gatekeepers matter for products chosen for someone else.** For children's services, COPPA
  requires operator name, address, phone and email in the privacy notice [B, 16 CFR 312.4(d)].
  No study covers parents reading About pages specifically [D].
- **Phones:** most About research is desktop. Treat the phone as the primary case for consumer
  products and desktop findings as a starting point.

## 4. The jobs it does, and the jobs it shouldn't take on

**Functional, for the visitor:** verify the maker exists and is who it claims; understand what
they do and for whom; find a human; collect facts for a task (journalist, investor, reviewer);
judge fit and terms ("how do they make money? will they sell my data?"). "Company motive"
appeared in 15.5% of credibility comments, 20.2% for nonprofits [A, Fogg 2002].

**Emotional:** less fear of loss (money, data, time: "will they still be around?"), and a sense
that a person, not a machine, stands behind it. Faces add social presence, which raises trust
indirectly [A, Cyr et al. 2009].

**Social:** "can I justify this choice to others?" (a boss, a partner, a parent), and "are these
my kind of people?" Identification is the route by which underdog stories work [A, Paharia et al.
2011]. For the sharer: could they defend the page if a friend asked? No study measures what a
weak About page costs the person who shared it [open].

**Maker jobs, legitimate:** be known for the work, meet legal duties, give press and partners
correct facts, recruit, set expectations with a business model and commitments, disclose
conflicts, be a clear entity for search and assistants, and tell why the thing exists.

**Jobs it wrongly takes on:**

- **Vanity and catharsis:** "our journey" written to be seen, not to answer. Personal material
  isn't the failure: longer, more self-disclosing Airbnb host profiles were trusted more, with
  diminishing returns [A, Ma et al. 2017]. Irrelevance to the reader is the failure.
- **Selling:** see section 1.
- **Keyword stuffing:** Google's spam policies name "blocks of text that list cities and regions"
  [B]. Keyword stuffing also did worse than baseline in generative engines, while added facts,
  statistics and quotations helped [A, GEO, KDD 2024].
- **Inflating scale for investors:** "global", "leading", logo walls. Investor material belongs in
  investor pages. Self-descriptions become evidence when a company fails (Builder.ai, 2025) [C].

## 5. Stories, faces, voice and AI: what the evidence says

- **Underdog stories work only under conditions.** They raised purchase intention, real choice and
  loyalty when they showed both external disadvantage and determination, through identification,
  most for people who see themselves as underdogs and when buying for themselves [A, Paharia et
  al. 2011, four studies]. Support fades when an underdog sells out [A, Siemens et al. 2020] and
  turns into betrayal after an ethical lapse [A, Kim & Park 2020]. A big brand adopting a small
  brand's warm voice reads as a poor fit [A, review 2021].
- **Founder stories about serving others read as more authentic** than stories about wealth or
  status, through "narrative fidelity": do the motives seem like good reasons for the actions? A
  merger weakened the effect [A, Hamby et al. 2019]. Make the motive concrete and plausible, not
  grand.
- **Stories persuade through transportation,** which needs an identifiable person, an imaginable
  moment and believable detail [A, Green & Brock 2000; van Laer et al. 2014 meta-analysis, 132
  effects]. A paragraph of adjectives isn't a story. Whether short web stories transport readers
  the same way is assumed, not tested.
- **Knowing a person made it adds value.** Handmade goods are valued more because they seem to
  "contain love", most for gifts [A, Fuchs et al. 2015]. The transfer to software or digital
  products made by a named small team is untested.
- **Modest self-disclosure raises liking** [A, Collins & Miller 1994, in person]. Transfer to
  one-way web text is assumed. Intimate or irrelevant disclosure isn't supported.
- **Faces add warmth, not proof, and can mislead.** Faces raised appeal and social presence, which
  raised trust, with no direct path and cultural differences [A, Cyr et al. 2009]. Photos of people
  made users *worse* at telling credible shops from weak ones: trust rose on weak sites and fell on
  strong ones [A, Riegelsberger et al. 2003]. People study photos of real staff and ignore stock
  people [B, NN/g 2010]. Undetected AI images scored no worse than stock, but suspicion of AI
  lowered ratings even for real photos [B, NN/g 2026-08-21, 77 people]. Use the real people with
  names and roles, or no people. The photo research is from 2002–2010, before generated faces were
  common.
- **"I" beats "we" in service talk.** Agents who said "I" were seen as more empathetic and willing
  to act, and drove more satisfaction and purchases, than agents who said "we"; customer-centred
  "you" had little effect or a negative one [A, Packard et al. 2018, over 1,200 interactions plus
  experiments]. Not tested on About pages, but it removes the main argument for a solo maker
  writing "we".
- **The visitor-or-maker question converges:** talk about the maker, chosen and framed to answer
  the visitor's doubts. Blue Acorn (2012): a "you"-centred About page gives "an About You" instead
  of the promised "About Us" [D]. Wiebe and Handley say the page must meet the prospect's
  objection [D]. No test data settles how far to lean.
- **AI authorship costs trust when suspected, most for emotional self-presentation.** People
  trusted Airbnb profiles less when they believed AI wrote them, in mixed pools [A, Jakesch et al.
  2019]. Emotional messages believed to be AI-written seemed less authentic, triggered moral
  disgust and cut word of mouth; factual messages and AI-assisted editing were penalised far less
  [A, Kirk & Givi 2025, seven preregistered studies]. 76% of US adults say telling AI from human
  text matters a lot; 12% are confident they can [B, Pew 2025]. A personal About page is exactly
  where the penalty is largest.

## 6. First principles

Each holds regardless of industry or size, and each names why.

1. **Answer before you tell.** The visitor arrives with a question: who, what, where, can I trust
   them, how do I reach them. Quick answers read as transparency, buried ones as evasion [B, NN/g],
   and an unnoticed fact has no effect [A, Fogg 2003]. Open with one or two plain sentences: who,
   what they make, for whom, where. Story comes after.
2. **Specific and checkable beats general and glowing.** Names, dates, places, numbers and
   registrations are costly to fake and easy to verify, so they work as signals. Adjectives cost
   nothing and are discounted. "Third-party sites are much more credible than anything you can say
   yourself" [B, Nielsen 1999]. Every claim is a checkable fact or links to evidence.
3. **Real, named people are accountable.** A named person can be found and held responsible, and
   scams hide identity "to prevent recourse" [B, rater guidelines]. Use real people or none. A
   pseudonymous maker stays consistent and contactable rather than faking a name.
4. **Agree with everything a visitor can check.** Visitors and raters cross-check stores,
   registers, reviews, profiles and press. Inconsistency reads as deception even when it's
   neglect. One legal name, one public name, one location, one founding year, everywhere; explain
   any difference ("sold on the App Store as Example Ltd").
5. **Match depth to the stakes.** A game or humour site can be short and warm. A shop, a paid
   app, anything handling personal data, health, finance, children or donations needs full
   identity, contact, policies and outside proof [A, Fogg 2002; B, Google].
6. **Say how you make money and who owns you.** An undisclosed motive, once found, undoes trust.
   One honest paragraph on funding, ownership, sponsors, affiliates and acquisitions answers "will
   they sell my data?" and "will they still be here?" [A, B].
7. **Interchangeable is untrustworthy.** If a competitor could sign a sentence unchanged, it says
   nothing, and it is what fraud shops copy [A, SCAMMAGNIFIER]. Replace it with a fact only this
   maker can claim.
8. **A modest true story beats a vivid false one.** Origin myths collapse when an insider, a book
   or a journalist checks, and the cost compounds for years (section 8, cases). Check every origin
   claim with the person who lived it and a second source.
9. **Tell the truth in a recognizable voice, including limits.** Admitting who it's not for and
   what went wrong is a costly signal of honesty [D, Bloomstein 2021; C, real pages]. Write as the
   maker speaks.
10. **Make it findable and label it plainly.** "About" in the header or footer of every page. Users
    fall back on the footer and avoid obscure labels [B, NN/g]. Contact help sits in the same place
    on every page [B, WCAG 2.2 SC 3.2.6].
11. **Lead to the next step.** The visit usually happens near a decision. End with what each main
    reader came to do: try or buy, reach a person, read the press kit, see jobs.
12. **Keep it current.** People, numbers and ownership go stale faster than any other content
    [B, Stanford guideline 8; C, Mailchimp's history]. Name the triggers for review (section 14).

## 7. The review

Run these on the rendered page at phone size first, then desktop, after the off-site check
(section 12). Score each pass, weak or fail, and note the fix.

**The two-minute skeptic test (on yourself, then with others).** Arrive as a stranger who found
the product a minute ago and wants to know if it's real. In two minutes, from the page and one
search, can you say: who is behind it, what they make and for whom, where they are, how to reach
a person, how it makes money, and has anything you found off-site contradicted the page? Each
"no" is a finding.

1. **Findable:** from the home page and two inner pages, an "About" link in the header or footer,
   plainly labelled.
2. **Who:** a named person, team or legal entity in the first lines.
3. **What and for whom:** cover all but the first two sentences. Can a stranger say what the
   maker makes and for whom?
4. **Where:** a location at the level the stakes and the law require (city or country at least).
5. **A human route:** email, form or phone visible or one click away, with a response expectation
   if possible.
6. **Interchangeability:** could a competitor sign each sentence? Cut or replace those.
7. **Checkable facts over adjectives** in the summary: dates, numbers, places, named people.
8. **Proof:** links out to independent evidence (press, registers, store listing, reviews,
   repositories, awarding bodies).
9. **Money and ownership:** one plain statement.
10. **People:** real and named, with roles. Reverse-image-search one photo. Names traceable
    elsewhere. No stock or generated faces.
11. **The story, if any:** a person, a moment, believable specifics, a motive that serves others,
    and true (checked against a dated source). Does it answer a reader's question? If someone
    other than its subject wrote it, has the subject approved every sentence?
12. **Depth matches stakes** (principle 5).
13. **Consistency:** name, entity, location, founding year, "I" or "we", and counts match the
    home page, press kit, FAQ, privacy policy, imprint, store listings and social bios
    (section 11).
14. **Personal details:** each one consented to and safety-checked (section 8, row 14).
15. **Testimonials and claims:** attributable, permitted, insiders disclosed, awards with their
    awarding body, nothing bought or implied.
16. **Current:** no departed people, aged numbers, dead links or past owners.
17. **Form:** headings alone tell the story; a one-to-two-paragraph summary on top; on a phone,
    the summary and key facts come before large images.
18. **Accessibility:** people photos have names in alt text or captions; headings in order; text
    contrast at least 4.5:1 (3:1 for large text); essential facts never only in images [B, WCAG
    2.2].
19. **Machines:** structured data (`AboutPage` or `ProfilePage`, with `Organization` or `Person`
    and `sameAs` links to real profiles) validates; the page has its own og:title, description and
    image; one plain self-description sentence reads correctly lifted out of context [B, Google].
20. **Next step:** the page ends with a relevant action for each main reader.
21. **Legal minimum, flagged not advised:** EU, German and Austrian commercial sites need an
    imprint reachable in two clearly labelled clicks [B, BGH 2006; § 5 DDG]; UK companies show
    registered name, number and office [B, SI 2015/17]; app makers' identity matches the store
    listings.

## 9. By who is behind the site

| Maker | The page must carry | It can skip | Strong examples (checked 2026-11-20) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Solo maker (app, game, tool, newsletter, craft) | The person's name or a stable pseudonym, a real photo or none, what they make and why, how long, where (to the level law requires), how it earns money, one direct contact | Org charts, corporate mission language | Ugmonk (founder letter, year, studio address, real photos, email); Simon Willison (specific bio, dated disclosures); marco.org (projects with dates) |
| Small studio | Named team with roles and photos, what they make and for whom, history, location, selected work, contact | Investor detail | Literature & Latte (founded 2006, company number and address, 12 named staff) |
| Startup | One-line what, founders, year, team location, funding, scale proof, jobs | Long history | Plausible (bootstrapped, dated milestones, exact counts, open source) |
| Acquired or mid-size company | Ownership, history, a founder voice or leaders, values with evidence | Detailed financials | Mailchimp ("now part of Intuit"); Basecamp (founder letter with his email) |
| Large company | What it does, scale, named leaders, governance and investor links, history, newsroom, contact | Personal story | Weak counter-examples: Microsoft (no leaders), about.google (product news first) |
| Nonprofit | Mission, who runs it, how money is used, audited financials, ratings, impact with method | Product marketing | charity: water; Wikimedia; MSF |
| Media | Ownership and funding, editorial standards, corrections, tips, leadership | Product marketing | ProPublica; The Trust Project's indicators as a checklist |
| Anonymous or pseudonymous | A stable pseudonym, a checkable track record, methods, a reliable contact, any legal identity commerce requires | A real name, with a legitimate reason | Gwern (revision history, confidence tags) |
| Local business | Name, address and hours exactly as on maps, the people, history, how to order | Investor detail | Tartine (founders, year, street corner) |

- **Solo makers:** write as a person ("I make this"), and describe real collaborators accurately
  (freelancers, contractors, family testers). Bias or safety concerns are reasons to share less,
  never to invent more. The Witchsy founders' invented male co-founder (2017) worked tactically and
  is still a fabricated person [C]. Name the bus factor honestly: what happens if the maker
  stops?
- **Solo makers and addresses:** raters accept a personal site omitting a home address [B], but EU
  and German law require a geographic address for commercial providers, and the stores publish
  trader and organization addresses. The choice is usually a home address against a business
  address service. Flag the conflict; the owner decides.
- **Corporate parents** state ownership plainly and can still tell a true story about a team, a
  place or a craft.
- **About pages track the company's stage.** Mailchimp went from "our staff of about 60 nerds"
  (2010) to 12 named leaders (2017) to an Intuit page with none (2026). Basecamp went from a facts
  sheet (1999, ~50 people, 32 cities) to a founder letter, and lost checkable facts on the way.
  Plausible's specifics grew with its proof [C, Wayback, checked 2026-11-20].

## 10. Patterns, and when each fits

Most good pages combine two or three. No pattern has controlled evidence that it converts better;
the "fits" column reasons from sections 5 and 6.

| Pattern | Fits | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Facts sheet (short summary + key facts) | Everyone, as the top layer; large companies and public bodies as the main pattern | Can feel cold; pair with people |
| Founder letter (first person, signed, with a contact) | Founder-led companies, solo makers, small studios | The founder's real voice and address; weak after a merger |
| Meet the maker | Solo makers, craft, indie developers | Personal exposure; check consent |
| Origin story | Makers whose readers share the struggle | Needs a scene and specifics; underdog framing only if true and current |
| FAQ-style About ("Who are you? How do you make money? Is my data safe?") | Products facing recurring trust questions | Keep answers short and factual (Pinboard) |
| Business-model statement | Free or cheap tools, privacy-sensitive products, media | Must match the privacy policy and pricing |
| Mission, values or manifesto | Mission-driven products, nonprofits | Each value needs evidence: a policy, a practice, a number |
| Team grid or leadership | Studios, companies, nonprofits | Real people only; maintenance and consent |
| Timeline | Older organizations | A summary on top |
| Proof and transparency | Nonprofits, public bodies, regulated businesses | Numbers need a source and date |
| Disclosures | Writers, reviewers, newsletters | Keep current, dated |
| Product-as-About | Games and single-product sites | Link to the maker or press kit |
| Imprint-only | The legal minimum in DE and AT | Satisfies law, not trust; pair with an About page |
| Corporate hub | Very large companies | Drifts into promotion that no longer says who is behind it |

## 11. The family of pages and the fact sheet

The About page owns the maker's identity and story. Other pages own their own jobs, repeat only
the facts their reader needs, word for word, and link back for the rest.

| Page | Owns | Repeats from About |
|---|---|---|
| Home page | What the product does and why to try it | A line naming the maker or kind of maker; a link to About |
| Contact or support | How to get help, response times | The same contact route |
| Imprint or legal notice | The statutory identity block | Nothing narrative; legal name and address identical |
| Privacy policy | Data practices; the controller's identity and contact (GDPR Art. 13; COPPA operators) | Legal name, address, contact, identical |
| Press kit | Fact sheet, boilerplate, images, bios, press contact | Founding date, founders, location, one-line description, identical; linked both ways. Not the same prose: the press kit is written for journalists |
| FAQ | Product and policy questions | "Who makes this?" in one line, linked |
| Blog | Updates | Bylines linking to About or author pages |
| Store listings | Product description; platform-verified developer identity | Developer name, legal name, address and contact must match |
| Social profiles | A short bio | Name, one line, location, link |
| Structured data and knowledge sources | Machine-readable facts | Name, founding date, founders, address, `sameAs` |

- **Give each fact one canonical home** and link to it. Copies drift apart. Copy-pasted press-kit
  prose keeps a journalist's register on a visitor's page.
- **Keep one fact sheet:** legal name and form, public name, the one-sentence description,
  founding date, founders, people and roles, location (public level and legal address), team
  size, how it earns money, ownership and funding, contact routes, platforms, store names and the
  countries it's sold in, counts used in copy (with dates), links to proof, and who wrote the
  page's first-person text and whether its subject approved it. Check every outlet against it at each
  review.
- **Store listings now show legal identity,** so a cosy first name on the website and an
  unexplained company name in the store is a trust gap. Use the same words, or explain.

## 12. Gathering on your own

Before speaking to the owner, gather everything a source can tell you. In rough order of value:

1. **The site as a visitor:** the About page rendered, then the home page, press kit, FAQ, support,
   privacy policy (controller), imprint, footer, and any founding blog post (often the best primary
   source for the origin).
2. **Store listings and platform profiles:** developer name, legal name, address, website and
   contact as the platforms verified them, the developer's other apps, and the countries each
   store sells in (they decide which disclosure laws and store displays apply).
3. **Registers,** where they apply: company, trademark, charity. They settle legal names and dates.
4. **The page's history:** Wayback snapshots, or earlier versions of the page's file, for what was
   said first and what was dropped. Read for the text, not for the reasons behind changes.
5. **The off-site check:** "[name]", "[name] -site:[domain]", "[name] reviews", "[name] scam", "is
   [name] legit", "who makes [product]"; social bios; press and interviews. Note products with
   similar names: telling the maker apart from them is part of the page's job, for people and for
   assistants.
6. **Reviews, forums and support threads:** recurring doubts ("is this one person?", "will it be
   abandoned?", "who's behind this?"). These are the questions the page must answer.
7. **Two or three AI assistants:** "Who makes [product]?" and "What is [maker] known for?" Record
   the answers, sources and date. Mismatches show which facts need a plain canonical statement and
   which outside sources need fixing. Don't promise the page controls what assistants say. If you
   can't reach other assistants, ask the owner to run the questions and paste the dated answers.
8. **Analytics,** if any: views, entrances and sources, previous and next pages, exits, device mix,
   site-search terms like "about", "contact", "who".
9. **Three to five peer About pages:** to know the category's conventions, and to avoid sounding
   like them.
10. **Earlier interviews, briefs and notes about the owner's story:** raw material. Extract the
    facts and quotes, mark each verified or not, list what's missing (scenes, dates, names,
    numbers), and note which questions were asked badly. People's accounts drift with retelling,
    and an interview that asked for opinions yields no scenes.

**Out of this step:** a draft fact sheet, a list of contradictions between sources, a list of
visitors' doubts, the facts only the owner can supply, and an interview guide.

## 13. The owner interview

**Facts by questionnaire, story by interview.** Facts gathered by interview drift; stories gathered
by questionnaire go flat. Send a short written list of facts to confirm first, so the interview
goes to scenes and judgment.

**Preparing.** Start from what you gathered. Where an earlier interview exists, re-ask its key
questions in a fresh way, aimed at incidents, rather than reading old answers back. Bring every
contradiction to resolve. Ask consent to record, say how notes will be used, and agree who approves
the text [B, Oral History Association 2025].

**Three blocks:**

1. **Confirm and resolve facts** (short): the contradictions, the canonical versions.
2. **Scenes and specifics** (most of the time). The critical incident technique asks for specific
   incidents, not opinions [A, Flanagan 1954]. Prompts: "Tell me about the day you decided to build
   this." "Walk me through the first time someone used it." "Tell me about a time you dropped an
   idea because it didn't fit." "What did you believe about this before, and what do you believe
   now?" "Who else was there?"
3. **Ladder to values.** After a concrete answer, ask "Why does that matter to you?" to move from
   what to why to the value behind it; stop when answers turn generic [A, Reynolds & Gutman 1988].

**Running it:**

- One open question at a time. Beginners ask yes/no questions that "only test your hypothesis"
  (Sawatsky), leading questions that "reveal your biases" (Baylor Institute for Oral History), and
  double-barrelled questions that let the subject answer the easier half (the most common mistake,
  per McLaughlin and Poynter) [D, B].
- Follow up: "How do you know that?" "Can you give me an example?" "What happened next?" "Describe
  that for me." "What do you mean by …?" [D, Emerson 2017].
- Use silence. People speak in paragraphs; waiting invites the next one [C, Portigal 2023].
- Ask the visitor's questions: "How does it make money?" "What happens to my data?" "What if you
  stop?" "Who answers email?" "Who is it not for?"
- Ask what must not be published: home address, children's names, health, disputes, deals.
- Don't accept the first answer as the story. The first answer is usually the rehearsed one.

**Out of the interview:** scenes, facts to verify, quotes to confirm, and private items to
exclude.

**After:** check every fact against an independent source; founding stories drift, and dated
primary sources (a first post, an archived first site, a registration) settle disputes. Send the
draft with every quote and personal detail marked for confirmation, and record the approval. News
journalism refuses quote approval to protect independence [B, NYT 2012]; an About page is
published by its subject, so review by each quoted person is the norm, as in oral history.

## 14. New page, edit, and the rebuild-or-revise call

**A new page, in order:**

1. A brief: the two or three readers that matter, the decision each is making, their doubts, and
   what the owner needs the page to do [B, Halvorson & Rach 2012; GOV.UK].
2. The pattern or patterns (section 10), by maker type and stakes.
3. Layers: a one-sentence statement (also usable as boilerplate and a search snippet), a one-to-two
   paragraph summary with key facts, then sections serving each reader, then next steps and links
   [B, NN/g's four levels].
4. The story, from the interview, in the owner's own words where they're vivid, chosen to answer a
   reader's question.
5. The fact sheet and structured data written with the page, so prose and markup agree.
6. Verification, the section 7 review at phone size first, the section 8 list, owner approval.
7. A maintenance list: the triggers below, the fact sheet, and who keeps them in sync.

**An edit, in order:**

1. **Inventory:** what's there, its history, inbound links, what visitors still ask.
2. **Audit:** mark each section keep, fix, cut or missing against section 7 [B-, Land 2023].
3. **Diagnose the kind of problem** before choosing the remedy:
   - **Facts** (wrong, stale, inconsistent): revise in place.
   - **Order** (good material, buried answers): restructure; summary and key facts first.
   - **Voice** (filler, superlatives, a press bio's register): rewrite sentences, keep structure.
   - **Purpose** (answers questions nobody has, is really a sales page or a memoir, or describes a
     business that has changed): rebuild from a new brief.
4. **Rebuild or revise follows from the diagnosis.** Rebuild when the purpose is wrong or most
   sections fail the audit. Otherwise revise, because existing pages carry links, search history
   and the owner's own words. Keep the URL; redirect it if it must change.
5. **Re-interview where the page is thin:** targeted for a revision, full for a rebuild.
6. **Done when** the audited failures are fixed and nothing that passed before now fails.

Line-editing without a diagnosis polishes the structural problems [D].

**Triggers for review:** someone joins or leaves; ownership, funding or legal entity changes; the
address changes; a number or milestone in the copy is passed or a new product is added; a store
listing or register entry changes; a new product changes "what we do"; a year has passed.

## 15. Measuring whether it works

- **No published controlled test isolates an About page** (searched 2026-11-20). Any "this About
  change lifted conversion X%" claim without a test design is unproven.
- **"About viewers buy more" is selection bias.** Blue Acorn's "5× more likely to buy, 22.5% more
  spend" (2012, ten clients) and Evogear's "+30%" (2006) compare people who chose to visit with
  people who didn't. People near a decision seek the page out. It shows the page sits on serious
  buyers' paths, not that it causes sales [D, C].
- **Controlled tests need more traffic than About pages get.** Detecting a 5% lift can need
  hundreds of thousands of visitors per variant [B, Kohavi et al. 2020]. Only high-traffic sites
  should try, on a downstream action, never on time on page.
- **Analytics traps:** time on page is ambiguous (reading looks like hunting for a missing phone
  number), and Universal Analytics gave the last page zero time. Rising views can mean more doubt.
  Exits can be satisfied or off to check elsewhere. Team compliments and design-gallery praise
  measure the makers' taste. "Nobody complained" means nothing: distrustful visitors leave
  silently [C, D].
- **What settles it:**
  - **Five-second test,** made for single-purpose content pages, which suits an About page: show
    it for five seconds, then ask who is behind it, what they make, where they are [C, Perfetti
    2005]. Use 5–10 people from the audience. Pass when 80% get who and what right.
  - **First-click test:** from the home page, "Where would you click to find out who makes this?"
    A right first click led to success 87% of the time, against 46% [C, Bailey & Wolfson 2009].
  - **NN/g's trust task:** "A friend is about to [use] this and asks whether it's a good company
    to deal with. Look at the site and tell them: good or bad? Why?" Then directed tasks: who runs
    it, when it started, how to reach a person. A good page's verdicts cite facts from the page
    [B].
  - **Consistency:** the fact sheet matches every outlet, search result and AI answer.
  - **Absence of identity doubts:** tag support emails, reviews and searches that ask "is this
    real?", "is it just you?", "will you keep updating it?" Falling counts after a revision are the
    best small-site evidence. Keep a dated log of page changes.
  - **One question to new users for a month:** "What made you trust it enough to try it?" and
    "What almost stopped you?"

## 16. Myths and claims checked

| Claim | Verdict |
|---|---|
| "52% of visitors want to see About first" | Misquoted: 52% of 262 self-selected B2B buyers (2014) wanted About *reachable* from the home page; 16% look there first [C/D] |
| "The About page is the second most visited page" | No study; one designer's client observation (2022) and an unmatched Chartbeat attribution [D] |
| "People don't read About pages" | Unsupported: few start there, many rely on it at the decision point [B, C] |
| "About visitors are 5× more likely to buy" | Real origin (Blue Acorn 2012, ten clients), causal misuse [D] |
| "A founder photo lifts conversion 35%" | A 2011 home-page banner for a founder already known from TV ads, no sample size; not an About page [C] |
| "Human photos double conversions (+102.5%)" | Highrise 2011: a whole-page redesign; did not repeat on Basecamp [C] |
| "Photos of people build trust" | Conditional: real, named, relevant people only; can blur credibility judgment [A] |
| "Stock photos kill trust" | Overstated: stock people are ignored, unless presented as the team, which makes them a lie [B] |
| "Use 'we' to look bigger" | Contradicted in service talk: "I" wins [A, indirect] |
| "Make the customer the hero" (StoryBrand) | Untested beyond the company's own cases; customer-centred "you" had little or negative effect [D; A indirect] |
| "Always tell your origin story" / "use the hero's journey" | Conditional: a true, specific, relevant story helps some readers; facts come first; a recognisable formula invites persuasion knowledge [A, C] |
| "Keep it short" | Half true: a short summary on top, substance behind; longer self-disclosing profiles were trusted more, with diminishing returns [A, B] |
| "Show your logos and awards" | Conditional: verifiable, permitted, linked; never bought or implied [B, C] |
| "A physical address is the strongest credibility signal" | Self-report only (second to quick support replies); rarely mentioned in real evaluations except for nonprofits [A] |
| "46% judge credibility by design" / "75% judge by design (Stanford)" | 46.1% of *comments*, 2002 data; the 75% is untraceable [A narrow / D] |
| "An About page boosts rankings" / "E-E-A-T is a ranking factor" | Overstated: Google says E-E-A-T isn't a ranking factor and rater data don't rank pages [B] |
| "You need llms.txt for AI assistants" | Google said (May 2026) its AI features don't use it; crawlers rarely request it [B, D] |
| "52% stop reading suspected AI content" | Self-reported "less engaged"; in the same survey's blind test, 56% preferred the AI article [D] |
| "An Impressum must be one click from every page" | Too strict: two clearly labelled clicks suffice (BGH 2006) [B] |

## 17. Open questions

- **Does a better About page cause more conversions?** No controlled test exists.
- **"I" or "we" for a solo maker,** once readers find out? No About-page test; consistency with
  store listings and support replies is the safer rule.
- **Story-led or facts-led for unknown small makers?** Untested.
- **How much do AI assistants rely on the maker's own page?** Vendor data only; changes fast.
- **Do people photos still help now that faces can be generated?** The photo research predates
  generated faces.
- **What does a weak About page cost the person who shared it?** No research.
- **Consumers on phones:** almost no About-page research.
- **When should AI help be disclosed** on a personal page? Unsettled; Google asks for disclosure
  where readers would reasonably expect it.
